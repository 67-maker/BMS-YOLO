Effect of oxidative atmospheres on thermochemical degradation of tobacco: 
Discriminating between oxidative pyrolysis and combustion

Cigarette ABSTRACT  
When heated up in oxidative atmospheres, lignocellulosic materials undergo complex series/parallel reactions of 
pyrolysis and combustion, sometimes leading to homogeneous or heterogeneous ignition. It is therefore chal-
lenging to draw a clear border between purely degradative and oxidation reactions. This work is part of a larger 
investigation on the role of oxygen in the patterns, chemistry, and kinetics of tobacco ’s thermochemical 
degradation. The topic is particularly interesting for the design of innovative tobacco products that heat tobacco 
instead of combusting it, but it is also relevant for thermochemical conversion of all lignocellulosic biomass in 
general. Experiments of thermochemical degradation of tobacco have been carried out at different heating rates, 
under inert and oxidative atmospheres, both by Thermogravimetry (TG)/Differential Scanning Calorimetry 
(DSC) and in a small-scale fixed bed reactor, with contextual analysis of reaction products. In the present work, 
attention is devoted to the thermal effects and formation of carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO 2). A 
methodology based on the comparison of mass loss, heat and gas release curves is applied to distinguish between 
reactions of oxidative pyrolysis and combustion. It is shown that below 300 •C oxidative atmospheres enhance 
volatiles abstraction, but heterogeneous combustion/ignition of the tobacco only occurs at temperatures above 
400 •C. Molecular oxygen present in the gaseous atmosphere around the biomass particles indeed facilitates 
rupture of the biomass macromolecular structure during heat-up, leading to the release of some volatile 
components.   
1.Introduction 
Lignocellulosic biomass is a mixture of cellulose, hemicellulose, and 
lignin with relatively small portions of minerals and extractives. The 
patterns and kinetics of biomass pyrolysis in an inert atmosphere have 
been the object of extensive research and modeling efforts in the liter-
ature [1–4]. Tobacco is a particularly complex lignocellulosic biomass 
with relevant contents of alkaloids (especially nicotine), pectin, pro-
teins, various sugars, organic acids, phenolic compounds, aldehydes, 
and aromatic hydrocarbons [5–8]; its pyrolysis has been extensively 
investigated in the context of the tobacco industry [9–19], as one of the 
reaction steps occurring during the consumption of combusted ciga-
rettes. Due to the relevance for consumer health [20–24], attention has 
been devoted to the physical and reactive degradation processes [9–15], 
as well as detailed analysis of the resulting emissions [16–19]. Impor -
tantly, knowledge developed in the context of tobacco science can be 
applied to the more general context of thermochemical degradation and 
combustion of lignocellulosic biomass. Cigarettes can be examined in the framework of the chemical reac-
tion engineering. Traditional cigarettes are characterized by the for-
mation of a hot combustion front (at the tip) with temperature levels 
reaching more than 900 •C [20], and a high-temperature pyrolysis and 
distillation zone behind the combustion front. Modern electrically 
heated tobacco products, which are based on controlled electric heating, 
the so-called Heated Tobacco Products (HTPs) [25–30], have completely 
changed the paradigm. Since combustion reactions are exothermic and 
lead to temperature increases and generation of undesired and harmful 
products, these devices are generally designed to heat tobacco to tem-
peratures below around 350 •C. This operating temperature has been 
shown to be low enough to avoid the onset of ignition and subsequent 
combustion, yet sufficient for the release of low-temperature devolati -
lization products of tobacco. Therefore, it is of fundamental importance 
to understand the thermochemical degradation processes occurring at 
the low-temperature thermal conditions of HTPs during use and to 
clearly establish the thermal conditions required to avoid the combus -
tion of tobacco for the development of novel HTPs. Discriminating

atmospheres of a biomass, such as tobacco, is far from trivial. This 
especially holds true when analyzing the thermochemical degradation 
and influence of oxygen at the thermal conditions relevant for HTPs. As 
the operation of HTPs involves heating rates from around 5–10 •C/min 
between puffs to up to around 500 •C/min during the pre-heating phase 
prior to product use [29,31] different experimental techniques (e.g., 
Thermogravimetry (TG)/Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) anal-

required to cover the different heating rates and conditions involved 
during the thermochemical degradation of tobacco relevant to HTPs. 
While it is of fundamental importance to distinguish thermochemical 
degradation processes from combustion in the context of HTPs, it is also 
very relevant in fuel science in general. Oxygen reaching the fuel sur-
faces during the heat-up/devolatilization stage can modify the rate and 
paths of thermochemical reactions, thus determining the heat release 
patterns and product yields, which would affect the temperature profiles 
in a system. These aspects are particularly critical and unexplored for 
oxy-combustion processes, where nitrogen is replaced by a carbon di-
oxide (CO 2)-rich atmosphere and oxygen concentration must be care-
fully tuned to guarantee the same ignition and flaming conditions as in 
conventional combustion. The effect of volatiles, such as CO2, on ther-
mochemical degradation reactions may also be relevant in the context of 
HTPs, in which pyrolysis of tobacco results in CO2 emissions in an ox-
ygen limited environment between puffs (even if the emissions are 
rather limited). 
Previous work [18,19;32 –34] emphasized that thermochemical re-
action patterns and kinetics must be considered carefully if molecular 
oxygen from the gaseous atmosphere can reach the fuel surface during 
the heat-up stage. Oxygen can in some cases promote the rupture of the 
macromolecular structure of a solid, facilitating the abstraction of vol-
atile matter in the early stages of heat-up, even though combustion of 
the solid fuel occurs in a later, separate stage. The term oxidative py-
rolysis was hence used to distinguish oxygen-enhanced thermochemical 
degradation from combustion, where the fixed carbon in the solid fuel 
ignites and burns in parallel with devolatilization, leading to uncon -
trolled heat up and thermal degradation of the fuel. In a previous work 
[33], the shapes of derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves and 
carbon monoxide (CO) and CO2 profiles from experiments in inert to 
oxidative atmospheres were compared to distinguish between pyrolytic, 
oxy-pyrolytic, and combustion processes for different solid fuels, 
including a bituminous coal, some plastics and two biomasses (Robinia 
Pseudoacaia and tobacco). Applying TG analysis alone to distinguish the 
different processes proved to be challenging for biomass because it is 
made up of several constituents and has a high oxygen content. CO and 
CO2 are produced both from pyrolysis and combustion, but at signifi -
cantly different levels, with the CO and CO2 yields resulting from py-
rolysis being only a fraction of the emissions resulting from combustion. 
In addition, the analysis carried out in the previous work was limited by 
the fact that heavy volatile species were not analyzed, and such con-
stituents may undergo secondary reactions and even oxidation reactions 
in the gas phase. 
In the present work, we build on previous experience and further 
develop the original method. A new methodology is proposed to identify 
regimes of pyrolysis, oxidative pyrolysis, or combustion of biomass. The 
methodology relies not only on experiments in TG but also on experi -
ments in lab-scale reactors, where it is possible to sample all the prod-
ucts and perform a complete mass balance. The methodology will be 
illustrated and demonstrated for the case of tobacco. 
2.Experimental 
2.1. Materials 
Reconstituted tobacco (RT), commonly available in tobacco shops, 
was used for the experiments. RT was extracted from HEETS tobacco sticks (variant “Amber ”) acquired from a tobacco shop in Italy. This 
material is made in the EU under authority of Philip Morris Products S.A. 
(Neuch katel, Switzerland). 
The composition of RT used in HEETS , known as cast-leaf tobacco, is 
even more complex than that of traditional tobacco, because it com-
prises a mixture of ground tobacco and additives [29], including 
approximately 15 % glycerin and minor percentages of cellulose and 
guar (Table S1 in Supplementary Information ). Fig. 1 shows an image of 
the cast-leaf tobacco sample contained within a single tobacco stick. 
Proximate and Ultimate analyses were performed using a LECO 
TG701 and a LECO CHN 628 according to the standard procedures 
ASTM D5142 (UNI 9903) and ASTM D 5373, respectively. The results of 
the analyses (average over 3–5 measures) are reported in Table 1. 
Additional tests were performed on samples of guar, cellulose, and 
glycerin provided by Philip Morris International (PMI, Neuch katel, 
Switzerland). 
2.2. Preconditioning of the cast-leaf tobacco substrate 
Preconditioning tests on cast-leaf tobacco samples were carried out 
with the scope of limiting moisture content variation. Samples were 
stored for at least 48 h at 22 ±1 •C and 60 ±2 % relative humidity (RH). 
Conditioning was achieved in a glass chamber with the addition of so-
dium nitrite salts to saturate the atmosphere. Temperature and humidity 
were monitored via digital sensors. 
2.3. TGA tests 
TGA was carried out using a Netzsch 409 TG-DSC apparatus equip -
ped with a train of Analyzers ABB-A02020 to detect CO, CO2, CH4, H2, 
and O2 in the evolved gas. Samples were loaded in amounts of about 10 
mg. The gas flow was controlled by Bronkhorst controllers and set at 
200 mL/min. TG tests were carried out in inert conditions, using 
chromatographic-grade nitrogen, with air, and with O2/N2/CO 2 mix-
tures (with oxygen concentration in the range 10–30 %). 
Two types of heating programs were applied in the TG:  
A. The sample was heated up from an ambient temperature to 110 •C 
with a constant heating rate (Hr, 2–40 •C/min) and held at 110 •C for 
30 min to release moisture. The sample was then heated up to 750 •C 
with constant heating rate and held at the final temperature for 30 
min.  
B. The sample was heated up from an ambient temperature to 750 •C 
with a constant Hr (2–40 •C/min) and held at this final temperature 
for 30 min. 
2.4. Fixed bed reactor tests 
Tests in either inert or oxidative atmospheres were also performed in 
a small quartz tubular reactor with a diameter of 2 cm and a length of 50 
cm [34]. The reactor was placed in a horizontal configuration and 
externally heated by an electric oven, as shown in Fig. 2.

2.5. Analysis of the products 
At the end of each test, the solid, liquid, and gaseous products were 
analyzed as follows:  
≡The solid substrate (char or ash) remaining after each test was 
weighed and analyzed with a CHN elemental analyzer for the 
determination of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and nitrogen (N) 
content.  
≡The condensate recovered was weighed and characterized by Gas 
Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) (Agilent GC 68090- 
MSD 5975C with DB-5 ms capillary column), Size Exclusion Chro-
matography (SEC) (high-performance liquid chromatography 
(HPLC) system HP1050 series equipped with a UV–vis diode array 
detector), and HPLC (Agilent 1200 Infinity series) analysis. A Karl 
Fischer method using a Mettler Toledo V20 Karl Fischer volumetric 
titrator was applied for determination of the water content. The 
water-free condensed product is referred to as “Liquid ” hereafter.  
≡The gas streams were first analyzed by a system of online analyzers, 
with further analyses performed by collecting batches of the gas 
downstream of the analyzers in inert Tedlar ® sampling bags, at 
different reaction times corresponding to different reaction temper -
atures. Bag contents were analyzed by means of a micro-gas chro-
matograph (micro-GC) Agilent 3000 and an HP5890A gas 
chromatograph, equipped with a 7515 Chrompack Al2O3/KCl 
capillary column and a Flame Ionization Detector (GC-FID) for the 
determination of light hydrocarbons (C1-C6) in addition to H2, CH4, 
CO, and CO2. An HP5700A gas chromatograph equipped with an 
8700 Alltech coaxial column and a Thermal Conductivity Detector 
(GC-TCD) was also used for the analysis of CO2, CO, H2, and N2 
species; the gases contained in the sampling bags were analyzed by 
ABB NOx analyzers.

3.Results 
The following paragraph reports the results obtained from:  
1- TG/DSC/EGA tests in inert and oxidative conditions. The mass loss, 
heat flow, and gas profile analyses of CO, CO2, and CH4 were 
determined to be able to discuss the differences between pyrolysis, 
oxidative pyrolysis, and oxidation events.  2- Reactor tests in inert and oxidative conditions. Temperature profiles, 
mass balance, and gas profile analyses of CO, CO2, and C2-C6 were 
determined to support the TG/DSC/EGA results.  
3- TG/DSC/EGA and reactor tests with different O2 concentrations and 
CO2-rich atmospheres. The mass loss, heat flow and evolved gas 
profiles (CO, CO2, and CH4) were compared to discuss the influence 
of O2 concentration and CO2 on the onset of pyrolysis and oxidation 
events. 
3.1. TG measurements 
3.1.1. Inert conditions 
Fig. 3 depicts TG and DTG plots under inert conditions of tobacco at 
different heating rates, while Fig. 4 shows the corresponding evolved gas 
profiles (CO, CO2, and CH4). The results of the tests carried out in N2 on 
the main additives present in RT sample, namely, guar, cellulose, and 
glycerin, are shown in Fig. 5. 
The analysis of tests carried out under inert conditions reveals that 
DTG patterns are very complex due to the occurrence of multiple re-
actions and the presence of several constituents and additives in the cast- 
leaf tobacco samples. 
Regarding the mass loss events, it can be observed that:  
≡Two mass loss events occur in the region between room temperature 
and around 100 •C, associated with release of moisture and bound 
water.  
≡A remarkable mass loss event is revealed by the DTG peak before 
200 •C. The analysis of evolved gases does not show any peak at this 
temperature, suggesting that the main products at this stage are not 
detected by the gas analysis and are condensable at room 
temperature.  
≡A second remarkable mass loss occurs between 200 and 400 •C, with 
the main DTG peak at about 290 •C. The gas analysis systems detect 
the release of CO2 and CO. These species cannot be regarded as 
combustion products because they are released in inert conditions. 
From Fig. 5, it can be observed that additives contribute to the mass 
loss in this temperature range; glycerin evaporation results in a DTG 
peak before 270 •C, while decomposition of guar and cellulose in 
DTG peaks at around 300 and 350 •C, respectively. Nicotine release 
also occurs in this temperature range [35].  
≡At even higher temperatures, three further reactive events can be 
observed close to 450, 500, and 550 •C. Notably, these events do not 
correspond to major weight losses but can be clearly appreciated by 
looking at the gas profiles, with CH4 peaking at 500 •C and CO at 450 
and 550 •C. Again, CO and CO2 released in this stage result from 
pyrolysis reactions and cannot be regarded as combustion products 
because they are released in inert conditions.

shows that neither glycerin evaporation nor guar decomposition is

anticipated (at ~ 330 •C) by the presence of oxygen and followed by a 
later stage of residual char combustion (at ~ 480 •C).

3.2.1. Mass balances for reactor tests 
The mass yields in solid, liquid, and gas from the reactor tests are 
shown in Fig. 10. The solids yield (Ys) includes char and ash and was 
obtained by recovering and weighing the solid residue in the fixed bed 
reactor at the end of the experiment. The gas yield (YG) was calculated by integrating the results of the online gas analyzer and micro-GC. The 
liquid yield (YL) reported in the figure was obtained by calculating the 
difference as: YL 100 – YH2O-YG –Ys (YH2O 13 % from moisture is not 
reported in the figure). However, it must be noted that liquid yields were 
measured from the weight of the flasks, and the results were in 
reasonable agreement (±10 %) considering the experimental weighing 
error. 
The solid yield under inert conditions is always around 25 %. Com-
parison of this value with the proximate analysis of Table 1, which in-
dicates a value of approximately 20 % for the sum of fixed carbon and 
ash, suggests that near-complete devolatilization of the tobacco samples 
in these tests. For the tests performed in air, the yield in the solid product 
drops to values around 10 %, close to the ash content of the tobacco (see 
Table 1), suggesting that combustion of fixed carbon occurred. The gas 
yield —and even more remarkably —the liquid yield increased moving 
from inert to oxidative conditions. 
A mass balance on carbon was carried out considering: the yields in 
solid/liquid/gas reported in Fig. 10, the carbon content of the solid 
residue determined from proximate analysis (reported in Table S2 in the 
Supplementary Information ), and the carbon loss associated with release 
of all the carbon-containing species detected by online gas analyzer and 
micro-GC. The distributions of carbon between solid, gas, and liquid 
products are shown in Fig. 11. 
In the reactor tests carried out in air after heating to 550 •C, the final 
carbon content in the solid and liquid products decreases, while the 
carbon released as gaseous product increases with a faster heating rate. 
This suggests that oxygen in the air oxidizes the char and part of the 
condensable products, resulting in the formation of gaseous CO and CO2. 
This will be discussed extensively in the later paragraphs.

73.3. Analysis of evolved gas 
The EGA tests includes profiles of CO, CO2, CH4, and H2 released 
during the TG experiments. Eq. (2–3) were applied to calculate the mass 
of tobacco that is transformed into CO, CO2, CH4, and H2 throughout the 
TG experiments. The values of MWs to be used in Eq. (1–3) for the TG 
tests deserve some comments. Notably, CO and CO2 could be either 
pyrolysis or oxidation products, in the case when oxidation reactions 
occur [33]. However, when CO and CO2 are released as pyrolysis 
products, the mass loss associated to each mole of CO and CO2 released 
is 28 and 44 g/mol, respectively. Therefore, the MWs of the CO and CO2 
to be used in Eq. (1–3) are 28 and 44 g/mol, respectively. The rate of 
mass loss curve obtained through Eq. (3) during a TG test in N2 is re-
ported as DTG gas and are overlayed with the DTG curves obtained from 
the weight measurements (Fig. 12). 
It can be observed that at low temperature (below 500 •C) the rate of 
mass loss calculated from the thermobalance (DTG) is much higher than 
the one estimated from gas analysis (DTG gas). Most of the mass loss is 
from volatilization of other O-HC compounds evaporated from the to-
bacco, including glycerin. Conversely, the DTG and DTG gas profiles are 
in very good agreement at temperatures above 500 •C. 
Under oxidative conditions, different cases can be considered: 
Pyrolysis hypothesis . CO and CO2 are pyrolysis products. In this case, 
the mass of the tobacco sample lost with one mole of CO and CO2 is still 
equal to the 28 and 44 g/mol. The MWs to be assumed in Eq. (2–3) are 
28 and 44 g/mol. 
Combustion hypothesis : CO and CO2 are the result of oxidation. If 
oxidation reactions engage the solid carbon, only 12 g of tobacco are lost 
for each mole of CO and CO2. In this case, the MWs to be assumed in Eq. 
(2–3) are 12 g/mol for both CO and CO2. 
Volatiles oxidation hypothesis : Pyrolysis generates volatiles that are 
then oxidized in the gas phase to produce CO and CO2. In this case, the mass loss associated with each mole of CO and CO2 is intermediate be-
tween 12 and 28/44 g/mol. 
Fig. 13 compares the rate of mass loss calculated from the weight 
measurement (DTG) and gas analysis (DTG gas) in the two extreme hy-
pothesis of 1) pyrolysis and 2) heterogeneous combustion. 
At low temperature (T D300 •C), the DTG calculated from the 
weight measurement is higher than the DTG gas calculated from the 
release of CO, CO2, CH4, and H2 under the assumptions that CO and CO2 
are pyrolysis products and that they are oxidation products. Evaporation 
of glycerin and nicotine also takes place at this stage. At higher tem-
peratures, the DTG gas (grey line in Fig. 13B) calculated by working out 
the profiles of released CO, CO2, CH4, and H2 (assuming that CO and CO2 
are oxidation products) shows a peak at 430 •C. The same DTG peak was 
observed in the thermobalance (red line in Fig. 13B). 
The same technique was applied to the results of reactor tests. In this 
case, a larger array of gaseous species was monitored, including CnHm 
with 2–6 carbon atoms and also molecular oxygen. Notably, for reactor 
tests, it is not necessary to formulate an a priori hypothesis of type A-B-C 
to assign CO and CO2 MWs in Eq. (2–3), because the consumption of 
gaseous oxygen engaged in oxidation reactions is included in the overall 
mass balance. The onset of oxidation can, in fact, be excluded or 
confirmed already by looking at the oxygen concentration profiles.

4.Discriminating between thermal and oxidative processes 
A logical scheme that can help discriminating between purely ther-
mally activated processes (evaporation and pyrolysis) and oxidative 
phenomena is proposed in Fig. 17. It depicts the simple case of a single- 
component fuel, so the DTG pyrolysis pattern under inert conditions is 
characterized by a single peak. 
In this relatively simple case, the logical path is to first compare the 
DTG curves obtained in inert conditions with those obtained in oxidative 
ones. If no differences are found, it can be concluded that oxygen in the 
atmosphere does not alter the course of pyrolysis. 
If the DTG curve is altered in an oxidative condition, it is evident that 
oxygen favors the rupture of the structure through a mechanism of 
oxidative pyrolysis at temperatures below the ignition temperature of 
the material or heterogeneous combustion at temperature above the 
ignition temperature. To distinguish between these two possibilities, it is 
useful to compare the derivative mass loss obtained by the thermoba -
lance (DTG curve) with that calculated from gas analysis (DTG gas) ac-
cording to Eq. (2). 
Hypothesis A (CO and CO2 are pyrolysis products) or hypothesis B 
(they are combustion products) was tested assuming that the MWs for 
CO and CO2 of 28 g/mol and 44 g/mol, respectively, for hypothesis A, or 
12 g/mol for hypothesis B as the oxygen in this case does not come from the tobacco material. 
The combination of anticipation of the DTG peaks in air, an overlap 
of the DTG and DTG gas curves under hypothesis A, and evidence of 
exothermic reactions can be interpreted as a pyrolysis process where 
thermal feedback from volatiles oxidation determines particles over-
heating and accelerates the rate of the pyrolysis, without altering the 
nature of bond breakage and the primary pyrolysis products. 
Conversely, an overlap of the DTG and DTG gas curves under hy-
pothesis B indicates the occurrence of heterogeneous reactions (above 
the ignition temperature). This phenomenon is also accompanied by 
severe exothermicity. Very sharp DSC peaks at these elevated temper -
atures indicate ignition and subsequent heterogeneous combustion. 
Finally, if the DTG curve from the thermobalance is higher than the 
DTG gas under hypothesis B, an oxidative pyrolysis mechanism can be 
assumed. This can also be accompanied by volatiles oxidation, which 
can be inferred by analyzing the reaction thermicity. 
Application of the logical scheme proposed in Fig. 17 to the case of 
tobacco is not straightforward because lignocellulosic biomasses contain 
multiple components and the DTG curves include multiple peaks. 
However, the logical scheme of Fig. 17 can still be considered in dis-
cussing and interpreting the results of the present work. The main results 
of TG and reactor experiments are summarized in Tables 3-4 for inert 
and oxidative conditions and discussed in subsequent sections. 
4.1. Inert conditions (Table 3) 
Below 400 •C, the DTG gas curves are lower than the DTG calculated 
by TG analysis, considering the weight loss. In this temperature range, 
the heavy hydrocarbon species are probably collected as condensed 
matter and therefore not detected by the gas analyzers. Glycerin and 
nicotine evaporation, as well as pyrolysis of guar and cellulose, also 
occur at this stage. At T F450 •C DTG gas and DTG are good agreement, 
suggesting that higher temperature pyrolysis generates mainly gaseous

mobalance (from DSC curves) and reactor (from temperature profiles) 
confirms that CO and CO2 released at this stage are merely pyrolysis 
products and do not result from combustion.

pyrolysis take place in this stage, with release of O-HC/HC species not

of oxygen on the patterns, chemistry, and kinetics of thermochemical 
degradation of reconstituted cast-leaf tobacco, typically used in heated 
tobacco products that are designed to heat tobacco instead of com-
busting it. This experimental campaign was designed to analyze the 
thermal degradation of tobacco under specific laboratory conditions and

Experiments were carried out on tobacco samples at different heating 
rates up to 550 •C, in both an inert atmosphere and air, using a TG-DS 
and a lab-scale fixed bed quartz reactor, with contextual analysis of 
typical reaction products. Particular attention was devoted to thermal 
effects and the formation of oxygenated gaseous compounds. The results 
obtained with the different methods are in good agreement, and the 
comparison revealed three important observations:  
≡Multiple stages of weight loss are observed under inert conditions. 
Below 400 •C the main emissions are condensable O-HC/HC species, 
resulting from vaporization processes or pyrolysis. At higher tem-
peratures, gaseous products including CO and CO2 prevail.  
≡In oxidative conditions at low temperature, condensable O-HC/HC 
species resulting from vaporization and pyrolysis are still the main 
products emitted. The liquid yield is enhanced, and O-HC/HC species 
release is anticipated compared to inert conditions. Some heat 
release is registered between 270 and 400 •C, with contextual in-
crease of CO and CO2 that is attributed to partial oxidation of vola-
tiles. Above 400 •C, a strong exothermic stage appears, which is 
related to heterogeneous combustion of fixed carbon (char).  
≡Oxidative effects above 250 •C are favored by increasing oxygen 
concentration in the gas mixture, while they are unfavored when N2 
is replaced by CO2. A logical scheme (Fig. 17) was provided for the comparative analysis 
of TG-DTG and EGA, with the aim of understanding the role of oxygen 
present in the atmosphere during thermochemical degradation. The 
analysis leads to the conclusion that vaporization, pyrolysis, and 
oxidative pyrolysis prevail at low temperatures; in other words, oxygen 
present in the atmosphere favors bond cleavage and anticipates the first 
pyrolysis stages. Above 270 •C, partial oxidation of volatiles in the 
constant flow of heated air contributes to sample overheating and 
further accelerates the pyrolysis reactions. Heterogenous combustion 
occurs only above 400 •C, as consistently demonstrated by the results 
from the different experimental setups applied in this study. 
In conclusion, the results of this work demonstrate that if recon -
stituted cast-leaf tobacco is heated to temperatures below 400 •C, the 
tobacco will not combust, and oxidation effects are restricted to the 
occurrence of oxidative pyrolysis and limited volatile oxidation 
reactions. 
Future work will report a more comprehensive analysis of condens -
able and heavy volatile species and address the kinetics of primary and 
secondary reactions upon heat up of cast-leaf tobacco in inert and 
oxidative atmosphere.

Boosting CO 2Electroreduction to CH 4via
Tuning Neighboring Single-Copper Sites
Anxiang Guan, Zheng Chen, Yueli Quan, Chen Peng, Zhiqiang Wang, Tsun-Kong Sham, Chao Yang,
Yali Ji, Linping Qian, Xin Xu, and Gengfeng Zheng *Cite This: ACS Energy Lett.2020, 5, 1044−1053 Read Online

ABSTRACT: We report single-atom Cu catalysts dispersed on nitrogen-doped
carbon by a nitrogen-coordination strategy. The presence of nitrogen enabled
good dispersion and attachment of atomic Cu species on the nitrogen-doped
carbon frameworks with Cu−N xcongurations. The Cu doping concentrations
and Cu−N xcongurations were well-tuned by the pyrolysis temperature. At a high
Cu concentration of 4.9% mol, the distance between neighboring Cu−N xspecies
was close enough to enable C−C coupling and produce C 2H4. In contrast, at Cu
concentrations lower than 2.4% mol, the distance between Cu−N xspecies was large
so that the electrocatalyst favored the formation of CH 4as C 1products. Density
functional theory calculations further conrmed the capability of producing C 2H4
by two CO intermediates binding on two adjacent Cu−N 2sites, while the isolated
Cu−N 4, the neighboring Cu−N 4, and the isolated Cu−N 2sites led to formation of
CH 4. Our work demonstrates a facile approach of tuning active Cu sites for CO 2
electroreduction to dierent hydrocarbons.
The greenhouse eect caused by excessive carbon
dioxide (CO2) emission into the atmosphere has
been attracting increasing concern. The electro-
chemical conversion of CO 2using sustainable energy sources
is regarded as one of the promising approaches to reduce the
continuous accumulation of atmospheric CO 2and generate a
supply of value-added chemical fuels to regain the carbon
balance.1−3Because of the intrinsic inertness of CO 2
molecules, the electrochemical CO 2reduction reaction
(CO 2RR) should rely on the development of optimal
electrocatalysts that not only enable ecient CO 2reduction
under ambient conditions but also allow the production of
deep reduction products beyond carbon monoxide or formic
acid with good selectivity, such as methane (CH 4) and
ethylene (C 2H4).4Among a wide range of material candidates
that have been investigated for CO2RR electrocatalysts, Cu-
based materials are known as almost the only category that can
electrochemically convert CO 2to hydrocarbons and alcohols
with good selectivities in aqueous solutions.5,6Substantial
research eorts have been focused on preparing Cu-based
CO 2RR electrocatalysts from dierent aspects, including
morphology,7crystal plane,8material composition,9alloying,10
as well as the local pH near the electrode surface.11
Methane, a common raw material for natural gas and a
substitute for gasoline, is the deepest CO 2reduction product
for the C 1pathway.12As it involves the transfer of 8 electrons
and 8 protons, the electroreduction of CO2toward CH4issubstantially challenging. For instance, Yang and co-workers
synthesized copper nanowires for CO 2RR and found the
selectivity was sensitive to the nanowire morphological
changes, among which the 5-fold twinned copper nanowires
exhibited the best selectivity to CH 4.4Wang et al.
demonstrated that Cu atoms supported by CeO2nanorods
could provide multiple oxygen vacancies to facilitate the
electroreduction of CO 2to CH 4.13Despite these develop-
ments, Cu atoms/ions are generally prone to oxidation or
agglomeration even under atmospheric conditions, resulting in
both low catalyst stability and selectivity degrading.14Recently,
single-atom catalysts have been investigated as heterogeneous
catalysts because of their unique electronic properties and
maximized atomic utilization.15For instance, many studies
have shown that single-atom metal-based nitrogen-doped
carbon (i.e., M−N−C, such as Co/Ni/Fe and Mn−N−C)
catalysts are attractive CO2RR electrocatalysts.16−24Using a
single-atom Ni−N−C catalyst, the maximum Faradaic
eciency (FE) of electroreducing CO 2to CO was reported
to be as high as∼99%.17In the past few years, several Cu−N−
C electrocatalysts25−27were also reported to electrochemically

reduce CO 2into CO at relatively less negative potentials.
Nevertheless, the M−N−C electrocatalysts can only reduce
CO 2via the two-electron reduction products of CO and formic
acid. Because the key step of producing deep reduction
products, such as CH4, is the generation of the*CHO
intermediate by protonation of*CO, this step needs to
overcome a large energy barrier. Therefore, CH 4is
preferentially produced at higher negative potentials.28,29To
date, there are few studies on the further reduction of CO 2to
hydrocarbons or alcohols catalyzed by single-atom M−N−C
electrocatalysts.
In this work, we developed a Cu,N-codoped carbon
nanosheet structure by atomically constructing the single-
atom distribution of Cu−Nxcoordination, with varying Cu-
doping concentrations. The obtained single-atom Cu−Nx
coordination structure enabled an eective CO 2RR catalytic
activity toward hydrocarbons, including CH 4and C 2H4. At a
high Cu loading density (4.9% mol), Cu atoms were close
enough to each other to enhance the C−C coupling, thus
resulting in ethylene as a main CO 2reduction product. When
the Cu loading density was reduced to 2.4% mol, the C 1pathway
was then favored and the main CO2reduction product was
tuned to methane, thus suggesting a tailorable approach for
constructing Cu-doped carbon framework for dierent CO 2RR
selectivities.
The fabrication of Cu,N-codoped carbon nanosheets is
schematically illustrated in Figure 1 . First, 1,3,5-benzenetri-
carboxylic acid, copper(II) acetate monohydrate, and L-
glutamic acid were used as original feedstock to synthesize a
Cu(BTC)(H 2O)3metal−organic framework (MOF, Exper-
imental Section in the Supporting Information). The crystal
phase and morphology of the as-synthesized MOF were
investigated by X-ray diraction (XRD, Figure S1a ), Fouriertransform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Figure S1b ), and
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Figure S2 ). Afterward,
the Cu(BTC)(H 2O)3MOF was mixed with dicyandiamide
(NH2C(=NH)NHCN) and then calcined at high temper-
atures, during which the organic ligands were pyrolyzed to
form N-doped carbon framework with Cu. Finally, the
excessive Cu nanoparticles were etched by acid, while the
nitrogen-coordinated Cu atoms remained in the carbon
framework. The obtained products were designated as Cu−
N−C-T(whereT= 800, 900, 1000, and 1100°C, indicating
the calcination temperature). For comparison, Cu(BTC)-
(H2O)3was also calcined at 900°C without adding
dicyandiamide to study the role of dicyandiamide in the
formation of the Cu−N xconguration in the obtained catalyst,
and the resulting sample after acid leaching was designated as
Cu−C-900.
The XRD patterns of all the Cu−N−C-T-BL samples
(where BL means before acid leaching) exhibited a series of
sharp diraction peaks at 2θof 43.3°, 50.4°, 74.1°, 89.9°, and
95.1°( Figures 2 a and S3), corresponding to the (111), (200),
(220), (311), and (222) crystal planes of the cubic Cu
structure (JCPDS 040836). After the acid leaching, the
obtained Cu−N−C-Tsamples showed only a broad peak
centered at 26.4°for the (002) plane of the hexagonal graphitic
structure ( Figures 2 a and S4).30The XRD results suggested
that all the metallic state of Cu was removed by the acid
leaching, as the Cu particles had weak interaction with the
carbon matrix and were dissolved in the acid solution. For
comparison, the XRD patterns of Cu−C-900-BL, Cu−C-900,
and nitrogen-doped carbon (N−C) samples are also displayed
(Figure S5 ), in which the diraction peaks of Cu and carbon
can be easily identied.

Cu−N−C-900-BL and Cu−N−C-900 samples consist of
interconnected foam structures with wrinkled and entangled

N−C-900 catalyst presented cross-linked and ultrathin folded
sheet-like features ( Figure 2 d). The high-resolution TEM
image conrmed that there were no observable copper
particles in the Cu−N−C-900 catalyst ( Figure 2 e), and the
selected-area electron diraction (SAED) pattern suggested
the noncrystallinity of the Cu−N−C-900 sample ( Figure 2 e,
inset). The high-angle annular dark-eld scanning transmission
electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image also showed that
no metallic copper aggregation on the ultrathin carbon
nanosheets ( Figure S6 ). The EDS elemental mapping revealed
the uniform dispersion of the N and Cu atoms ( Figure 2 f).
The role of nitrogen doping was studied by comparison with
samples undoped with N. All Cu−N−C-T-BL and Cu−N−C-
Tcatalysts showed similar morphology to those of the N−C
samples ( Figures S7−S9 ), without obvious copper particles on
the surface of carbon sheets. While without adding nitrogen,
the Cu−C-900-BL sample retained the morphology of the
starting material Cu(BTC)(H 2O)3MOF with lots of copper
microspheres on the surface ( Figure S10 ), which disappeared
after treatment with oxygen-saturated acid ( Figure S11 ). The
TEM, elemental mapping ( Figure S12 ), and HAADF-STEMimages ( Figure S13 ) of all Cu−N−C-Tsamples were also used
for further conrmation. All these results indicated that N
atoms played a crucial role in stabilizing Cu atoms and
prevented them from agglomeration during the high-temper-
ature pyrolysis process, leading to a uniform distribution of Cu
single atoms in the carbon matrix.

C-900 sample. This signicantly reduced distance value

Catalyst stability during the CO 2RR reaction is an important
factor for industrial application, which can be inuenced by the
pH change of electrolyte,11,48the deposition of impurities from
the solution,49the structure change of the catalyst, and so on.50
Therefore, the stability of the Cu−N−C-900 catalyst was
studied under the electrochemical reduction reaction. The
current density showed no obvious attenuation after 10 h of
continuous electrolysis at−1.6 V vs RHE ( Figure 5 e). The
detection of gas products conrmed that the Cu−N−C-900
catalyst maintained a stable production rate for CH 4formation
(FE≈35.6% after 10 h of electrolysis) during this process.
After the CO 2RR, the XPS spectra of all the Cu−N−C-T
samples ( Figure S29 ) revealed that the N species and the
valence state of Cu sites remained almost unchanged,
indicating their robustness during the CO 2RR process.
Moreover, the XAS measurement was further conducted to
probe the structural and oxidation states of the Cu−Nx
congurations after the CO2reduction reaction. The Cu K-
edge XANES spectra ( Figure S30a ) of the Cu−N−C-800 and
Cu−N−C-900 catalysts after CO 2RR veried that Cu atoms in
these samples were still in the oxidation states of Cu(I) and
Cu(II), similar to those before the CO 2RR. The corresponding
EXAFS spectra ( Figure S30b ) after the CO 2RR also
demonstrated that Cu atoms existed as Cu−N xspecies in
the samples with the characteristic peak at 1.45 Å, and without
any Cu−Cu peak at around 2.24 Å,38further conrming the
excellent stability of these catalysts toward the electrochemical
reduction of CO2.
A mechanism was then proposed to illustrate why the
selectivity of CO 2electroreduction varied with the pyrolysis
temperature of catalysts. First, as shown in Table S2 , all the
Cu−N−C-Tsamples with no obvious functional groups on the
surface exhibited the same morphological features and similar
BET surface areas, indicating that these parameters were not
the critical factors that inuenced the selectivity of the CO 2RR.
However, the Cu contents for all catalysts were measured
(Table S3 ), and the results showed that the contents of Cu
decreased gradually with the increase of temperature. When
calcined at high temperature, some copper atoms coordinated
with nitrogen atoms were remained in the carbon frameworks
with Cu−Nxcoordination structure, while the other copper
atoms were detached from the carbon matrix and converted
into metallic copper. With the further increase of calcination
temperature, more and more copper atoms were removed from
the carbon networks and aggregated into nanoparticles, along
with the evaporation of nitrogen. As shown in Table S4 , the N
concentration gradually decreased with increasing pyrolysis
temperature for the Cu−N−C-Tseries, and the Cu−N−C-900
catalyst possessed a similar N concentration to that of the N−
C sample because both of them were pyrolyzed at the same
temperature. The Cu−C-900 catalyst showed the lowest N
concentration because of a lack of external nitrogen source
during its synthesis procedure. These results suggested the
successful doping of nitrogen into the carbon frameworks, and
the nitrogen concentration decreased at high temperatures
because of its volatilization.
As when a*CO intermediate is produced on one Cu site, it
either desorbs to form CH 4by following the pathway*CO→
*CHO→*CH 2O→*CH 3O→CH 4or couples with another
*CO intermediate on the neighboring single-atom Cu site to
form C 2H4through a C−C bond-formation step.51The Cu−
N−C-800 catalyst shows a more favorable selectivity toward
C2H4formation than that of CH 4, which is ascribed to theexistence of abundant single-atom Cu active sites and the
synergistic eect between them to induce the C−C
coupling ( Figure S31 ). At pyrolysis temperatures higher than
800°C, more and more atomic Cu species detach from the
carbon frameworks, and the remaining single-atom Cu species
are isolated from each other, leading to the increase of CH 4
products and the decrease of C2H4products ( Figure S32 ).
However, the more drastic pyrolysis treatment at a higher
temperature (1100°C) resulted in the deterioration of CO 2RR
activity and enhancement of the HER side reaction, as a vast
majority of active sites (Cu single atoms) for CO 2RR were
dissociated from the carbon framework at high temperatures.
Therefore, more and more naked carbon framework without
copper single atoms was obtained and thus became an inert
catalyst for CO 2electroreduction but showed increased HER
activity.52Thus, it can be concluded that the catalytic
selectivities of our catalysts are strongly related to the contents
of monodispersed Cu atoms on nitrogen-doped carbon with
the Cu−N xconguration. In our work, when the Cu
concentration reaches∼4.9% mol, the Cu single atoms are
close enough to each other, which leads to the presence of a
synergistic eect between adjacent Cu single atoms and favors
the dimerization of*CO intermediates. At a lower Cu
concentration below 2.4% mol, Cu single atoms are isolated
suciently far from each other, thus resulting in low surface
coverage of*CO species and enhancing the formation of CH4.
To further conrm our proposed mechanism, density
functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to
demonstrate the product selectivity of CO 2RR on dierent
Cu−N xcoordination structures ( Figures 5 f and S33). The
reaction procedures on the isolated and neighboring Cu−N4
are both thermodynamically uphill ( Figure 5 f), suggesting
inecient electrochemical CO2RR performance. The gentle
upslopes belonging to the CO binding strengths on the
isolated Cu−N 4and the neighboring Cu−N 4are both weak
and unable to favor the C−C coupling, suggesting that these
two sites prefer to produce C 1products. On the other hand,
the CO binding strengths on the isolated Cu−N 2(−2.36/2 =
−1.18 eV) and the neighboring Cu−N 2(−2.34/2 =−1.17 eV)
are both obviously stronger than that on the isolated Cu−N 4
(−0.20 eV) and the neighboring Cu−N4sites (−0.38/2 =
−0.19 eV), giving a strong indication that these two sites can
facilitate further CO reduction. In addition, OC−*COH (*
denotes the binding site) is known to be a common
intermediate proposed in the literature for the formation of
C2or C 2+products.53,54As shown in Figure S33i,j , the free
energy of C−C coupling on the isolated Cu−N 2is as high as
1.38 eV, while that on the neighboring Cu−N 2is only 0.14 eV.
This is consistent with our experimental observation that a
high Cu concentration site enables the C−C coupling to
produce C2H4, whereas a low Cu concentration site is unable
to play such a role.
In summary, we have demonstrated a temperature-tuned N-
coordination strategy to disperse a high content single-atomic
Cu on nitrogen-doped carbon for CO 2electroreduction. Our
results show that nitrogen could anchor single-atomic copper
species (up to∼4.9%mol) on the carbon frameworks with the
Cu−N xconguration, which was tailored by altering the
calcination temperature. These Cu−N xspecies were conrmed
to be the main active sites for CO 2RR. In addition, the
neighboring single-atomic Cu sites were in favor of C 2H4
formation, while isolated single-atomic Cu functioned as active
sites for CH 4production. Thus, by tuning the pyrolysisACS Energy Letters http://pubs.acs.org/journal/aelccp Letter

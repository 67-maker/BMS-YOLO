In this work, a full-loop 3D computational fluid dynamics (CFD) numerical simulation of the chemical looping 
gasification (CLG) process of lignite in a circulating fluidized bed (CFB) was performed using a two-fluid 
Eulerian-Eulerian model. The results showed that the CFD simulation effectively captured the axial and radial 
velocities of the particles. More radial flow occurred in the bottom part of the riser and inside the cyclone. The 
exothermic reaction became dominant within 7s, while the heat-absorbing reaction became dominant between 7 
and 15s. The aggregation regions of syngas moved up with that of the high-temperature zone, and CO was the 
main contributor to the syngas. The particle size in the range of 175–225 μm was inversely proportional to the 
size of the average heat transfer coefficient (HTC) in the riser. Extremely small particle size saturated the bed, 
suppressing the HTC. The peak of the HTC was 387.7 W/(m2⋅K), which occurred in the upper-middle region of 
the riser and reached. The maximum HTC average value was 180.6 W/(m2⋅K). Finally, experimental data were 
used to verify the reliability of the model.   
1.Introduction 
The issue of high carbon emissions has attracted considerable 
attention due to its contribution to the frequently increasing global 
climate change. Human activities, particularly the reliance on fossil 
energy sources (e.g., coal, oil, and natural gas), are the major, contrib -
utors to carbon emissions. In China, coal resources are abundant and 
easily accessible through mining. The perennial dependence on coal 
resources made its share of total energy high at approximately 70 % [1]. 
Because of the irreplaceability of coal resources in the energy structure 
at the present stage [2], the efficient and clean utilization of coal is 
crucial for realizing sustainable energy production. Lignite is, a potential 
coal resource that has attracted attention, especially as high-quality coal 
resources become more scarce due to extensive mining [3,4]. Lignite is 
known as inferior coal compared with other coal sources, and it is 
characterized by high volatility and good reactivity [5]. Some researchers have used lignite to prepare syngas through direct gasifi -
cation [6]; however, the strategy has, not achieved a better carbon 
conversion rate due to insufficient oxygen supply required for conven -
tional coal gasification reactions and expensive air separation units, 
limiting the development of the technology [7]. As a new gasification 
technology, chemical looping gasification (CLG) can rationally utilize 
the lattice oxygen in an oxygen carrier (OC) to enhance the gasification 
process of lignite [8]. Moreover, OC can often be prepared using rela-
tively inexpensive artificially synthesized materials or solid waste, 
substantially reducing production costs. Currently, CLG technology is 
frequently used for the efficient and clean conversion of coal and 
biomass [9–11]. CLG is also regarded as a more effective and environ -
mentally friendly approach with unique advantages in carbon capture 
and utilization [12]. Relevant literature has shown that good gasifica -
tion performance is better achieved with high-quality coal, coal coke, or 
biomass [13–15]. The use of inferior coal (lignite) is challenging due to

high energy and cause the environmental pollution. The high efficiency 
and environmental friendliness of CLG technology provides new possi -

technology of utilizing inferior coal can improve the current energy 
structure and deepen the reform of the energy supply, thus contributing 
to the world’s goal of “carbon peak” and “carbon neutrality”. 
The CLG process of lignite could be realized in many devices. Fixed, 
moving, and fluidized beds are commonly used for the CLG process of 
lignite [16]. A fluidized bed has been considered a recommended re-
action device for coal CLG owing to its excellent nonhomogeneous 
mixing efficiency, uniform temperature distribution, wide fuel adapt -
ability, and large potential for industrial development [4]. Although 
circulating fluidized bed (CFB) systems have been in operation for de-
cades, their experimental methods still present inherent drawbacks due 
to the complex and hostile environments present in CFB [17–19]. More 
detailed information on CFB is not available because of the constraints of 
the available measurement means. The reaction flow and the mass and 
heat transfer mechanism within CFB have not been fully understood. 
The lack of research on these principles led to many uncertainties in the 
industrial scale-up of CFB systems. It also hampered the optimal design 
and operation control of the CFB system, thereby, preventing the further 
development of coal CLG technology relying on CFB reactors. With the 
iterative updating of numerical simulation computational methods and 
advancements in computer hardware equipment, CFD simulation has 
become one of the extremely advanced and cost-effective tools to study 
the CLG process in the fluidized state [20]. 
Su et al. [21] performed two-dimensional (2D) CFD modeling of a 
self-developed coal-fired chemical looping combustion (CLC) 
double-CFB system. The simulation was used to optimize the operation 
process and improve the combustion efficiency. The 2D CFD model was 
developed using the finite volume method reported by Harichandan 
et al. [22]. The model was used to study the hydrodynamics of gas 
bubbles in the fuel reactor (FR) of the CLC system. CaSO 4 and H2 were 
used as OC and fuel, respectively. CFD simulations effectively captured 
the entire life cycle of the bubble from generation to bursting. Jung et al. 
[23] proposed a multiphase hydrodynamic model for the CLC FR process 
based on particle temperature kinetic theory. A simulation of the gas 
production in the FR showed the presence of methane fuel in the flue gas 
and high mass fractions of H2O and CO2. The methodology of the 
simulation considered high fuel losses in the FR. However, the above 
research used a simplified two-dimensional simulation, and the effect of 
radial flow inside the reactor on the reactive flow was not understood. 
As a result, all the characteristics of the fluid were not well explained. 
Even though 2D simulations have proven to be effective in some in-
vestigations for CFBs containing complex reaction kinetics and fluid-
ization structures, 2D simulations may often only be used as a pre-test 
for 3D simulations. 
Cornej et al. [24] used the two-fluid model (TFM) model to simulate 
the coal gasification process in a pilot fluidized bed reactor (riser). The 
model covered drying, volatilization, and gasification processes. Chen 
et al. [25] devised a Euler-Euler model to investigate the coal/biomass 
co-gasification. Collisions of multiple particle components were incor -
porated into the chemical reaction model considering multiphase flow. 
Dong et al. [26] conducted a CFD study for biomass in-situ gasification 
in a CLC system. They modeled FR in the CLC system using the multi -
phase particle intracellular method. The gas-solid reaction flow during 
biomass gasification was investigated. Ramakrishnan et al. [27] used 
CFD simulations to analyze the gasification of three Australian coal 
samples in a fluidized bed to provide a basis for designing industrial coal 
gasification reactors. The above literature reports mainly focused on the 
riser of the fluidized bed, lacking the examination of the full CFB cir-
culation loop. In actual operation, all parts of the CFB components affect 
the fluidization in the riser, thus affecting the stabilization of the whole 
reaction unit [28]. 
We preliminarily studied coal gasification in a riser. The FR reaction kinetics CFD model was developed, which was used to predict syngas 
preparation from coal CLG in riser [4]. However, only the gas-solid flow 
in the riser was considered in the study. Moreover, the kinetic model was 
relatively simple, making its predictions less accurate. In another one of 
our studies, we used more complex reactor structures and multi-step 
chemical reaction models [29]. A sensitivity analysis of the lignite 
CLG process was performed to derive reaction conditions for efficient 
syngas preparation. Even with the improved prediction of results and 
detailed information on multiphase flow, the study only identified the 
underlying reaction conditions. The flow structure inside the reactor 
was not comprehensively explored. The simulations were of a 
quasi-steady-state result, which did not consider the reaction charac -
teristics within the reactor over time. Moreover, most studies mainly 
focused on the macroscopic motions of the gas and solid phases and the 
preliminary exploration of the underlying reaction conditions. However, 
detailed exploration of the spatial and temporal distributions of the 
components, heat transfer coefficients, and the coupling mechanism 
between the multi-step reaction mechanisms have rarely been reported. 
This gap has fully limited the understanding of the complex gas-solid 
reaction flow mechanisms in CFBs. Therefore, a comprehensive and 
detailed investigation of the CLG process of lignite in CFB is necessary 
for a better understanding and application of this complex multiphase 
flow system. 
In this paper, the CFB was modeled with FLUENT using a Euler-Euler 
transient full loop 3D model. The gas-solid flow in the full loop within a 
CFB was effectively simulated to analyze complicated multiphase flows. 
The FR portion of the CLG process was evaluated, and the spatial and 
temporal profiles of components and the thermal properties of the gas- 
solid were investigated. Gas-solid temperatures and gas production at 
different times were also obtained to comprehensively analyze the 
mechanism of the multi-step reaction model. Moreover, the effect of 
different particle sizes on the localized heat transfer coefficient (HTC) in 
the riser was compared. The experimental data were used to confirm the 
efficacy of the model. The results of the numerical simulation will 
contribute to the stable operation of the CFB reactor. Meanwhile, opti-
mizing the lignite CLG process in the CFB reactor is crucial, providing a 
theoretical basis for the industrial operation of lignite CLG. 
2.Methodology model 
2.1. Details of the experimental process 
Our previous experimental data were used for the simulation in this 
study [29]. In this work, the FR for CLG was focused. The function of the 
FR was analyzed using a CFB developed by our research group. The CFB 
comprised a riser, cyclone separator, and return system. The structure of 
a CFB reactor with geometry is shown in Fig. 1. The reaction occurred in 
the riser. The cyclone performed favorable gas-solid separation. An air 
supply system supplied the fluidized gas in the riser and the return air 
from the leg. The height of the riser was 1.28 m, the diameter of the thick 
tube was 60 mm, and the diameter of the thin tube was 50 mm. The 
length of the conveyor tube to the cyclone was 0.415 m, and it had a 
diameter of 50 mm. The outlet diameter was 50 mm. The cyclone 
separator diameter was 110 mm. The downcomer diameter was 25 mm. 
H2O was mixed with N2 in a certain ratio and passed to the inlet, and N2 
was passed through the return leg. N2 was used to assist the water vapor, 
which could provide a stable fluidizing wind. It was also applied to 
balance the water vapor content. The electric heating was used to 
maintain a constant wall temperature. The walls of the riser provided 
the heat needed for the reaction. During the experiments, the side walls 
of the reactor had an insulating layer that provided general insulation 
from the external environment. There were several pressure monitoring 
points in the riser to detect pressure changes in real time. The gas from 
the bifurcation tube was dried with silica gel particles and passed into 
the flue gas analyzer for gas component analysis. We modeled a small 
CFB. The size was an experimental scale reactor. The controllability and W. 2.2. The CFD model 
The most common objects of study in CFD simulations are two types 
of multiphase flows: liquid-solid and gas-solid. CFB boilers usually 
involve gas-solid two-phase flow. Two simulation methods, namely 
Euler-Euler and Euler-Lagrange, are commonly used [34]. The 
Euler-Lagrange approach has been used by many scholars owing to its 
more accurate tracking of particles. However, stringent computational 
requirements have limited the application of the method. Especially for 
industrial-scale simulations, numerous particles significantly increase 
the cost of the simulation [35]. In the TFM model, the gas and solid 
phases are considered continuous media in the Eulerian framework [36]. A form similar to the N–S equations is in this model, with each 
phase described by a separate conservation equation [4]. The interphase 
interactions are characterized by interphase coupling, which will 
significantly reduce the computational cost. Moreover, related studies 
have showen that the accuracy of the Euler-Euler model is also sufficient 
for most of the simulation requirements. Some scholars have used the 
Eulerian-Eulerian method to simulate the solid-gas reaction in a CFB to 
achieve accurate results [37,38], particularly when simulating a dual 
circulation fluidized bed reactor [21,39]. It is not very likely that the 
Euler-Lagrange model will replace the Euler-Euler model. Currently, the 
Euler-Euler method is usually more popular [40]. The information on 
the Eulerian multiphase and the combustion model was also summa -
rized in detail in our previous work [29]. The kinetic theory of granular 
flow was used to model the motion of particles [41]. This simulation 
method has been recognized for predicting localized heat HTC in a 
fluidized bed riser [42]. To simplify the calculations and save compu -
tational costs, we assumed the solid-phase particles to be spheres of 
inelastic and monodisperse particles. The relevant model equations 
involved in the simulations are listed in Table 3.

3.Results and discussion 
3.1. Analysis of typical flow patterns in CFB 
3.1.1. CFB full loop particle concentration distribution analysis 
Given a more in-depth investigation of the interphase interaction 
mechanisms of gas-solid and solid-solid phases in gas-solid CFB, it is 
vital to pre-investigate the fluidization behavior of particles in the 
reactor. The result of the investigation is used to provide a theoretical 
basis for thermal state simulation. Fig. 5 shows the concentration profile 
of solid particles over time in the full CFB circuit. As shown in Fig. 5, as 
the fluidized gas continued to flow through the inlet of the riser, the 
particles in the riser formed agglomerates. Due to the rapid carrying 
action of the fluidized gas, the bed expanded and rapidly broke up to 
form fast fluidization [57]. This occurrence indicated that the particles 
were violently mixing in a collision that favored the exchange of mo-
mentum. In the early stage, the fluidized gas quickly transferred most of 
the particles to the higher part of the riser, and a small portion of the 
particles directly entered the downcomer from the return loop. Over a 
20-s dynamic process, the particles in the downward region of the riser 
transitioned from an initial buildup to sparse and then to dense. This 
transition was because most of the particles were rapidly transferred to 
the upper part of the riser and the return loop, resulting in an initial 
particle vacancy at the bottom of the riser. However, the particles were 
separated in the cyclone and recycled into the riser by the return circuit. 
The lower zone of the riser transitioned into a dense phase because the 
particles were recharged. In the middle part of the riser, there were 
intermittent “empty periods ”, and the distribution of particles in the 
riser was divided into two parts. This phenomenon was because the 
fluidization of the agglomerates was complex, and the gas-solid fluid-
ization movement was unstable. At 8–20 s, the distribution of particle

3.1.2. CFB full loop pressure distribution analysis 
Fig. 7 shows the temporal and spatial distribution of the pressure. At 
0.3 s, the pressure was maximum and was particularly dominant in the 
lower region of the CFB, which was attributed to the expansion of the 
dense bed that built up at the bottom when the gas suddenly surged from 
the inlet into the interior of the reactor. A gas blockage-like phenomenon 
briefly developed in the lower region of the reactor, which caused a 
rapid increase in pressure. Meanwhile, friction also played a significant 
role in the initial stages of reactor operation, which led to significant fluctuations in pressure. This phenomenon was also shown in the study 
of Ghadirian et al. [28]. However, this phenomenon appeared briefly in 
the fast fluidized bed, and the pressure became moderate after 0.3 s due 
to the rupture of particulate agglomerated bubbles after 0.3 s. The 
rupture of the bubbles allowed the gas to flow relatively unimpeded 
inside the reactor. Moreover, as the rapid fluidization continued, no 
bubble regeneration was observed in the reactor, and there was no 
sudden surge or precipitous drop in the pressure. The pressure in the 
riser was lower than the pressure in the return circuit until 4 s. The 
pressure difference caused some particles to enter the return circuit 
directly from the bottom of the riser. The pressure difference between 
the riser and the return circuit became smaller over time. After 8 s, the 
pressure in the return circuit increased more than the pressure in the 
riser. The granular material in the return circuit was pressurized by the 
pressure difference to the return port and entered the riser to form the 
return cycle; thus, the whole system formed a benign cycle of 
fluidization.

3.2.1. Thermal simulation of transient temperature analysis 
Because of the heating system and the occurrence of reaction in the 
riser, only the temperature distribution inside the riser was examined. 
Fig. 9 shows the distribution of particle temperatures in the riser. At 1s, 
the temperatures of particles in the lower region were close to room 
temperature, and the temperature of the particles in the upper part was 
extremely low. Then, the temperature of the particles in the lower region 
rapidly increased at 3–5 s. This rapid increase in the temperature was 
because the particles were densely piled up in the lower bed at 1 s and 
did not expand and rupture. At that time, the temperature provided by 
the wall surface could not be transferred to the internal particles in time, 
leading to relatively high temperatures only on the side wall particles. 
After 2 s, the dense bed broke up, and the collisions between particles became violent. As a result, the heat from the wall surface rapidly 
transferred to the particles. At the constant collision and remixing, the 
heat transfer efficiency between the particles also dramatically 
increased, thus rapidly increasing the temperature in the lower region. 
At 7–15 s, the high-temperature region gradually shifted to the upper 
region of the riser, thus decreasing the overall temperature in the 
furnace. This phenomenon was due to the nonhomogeneous carbon 
reaction controlling the bed and the increasing bed temperature. As coal 
particles were gradually transported to the higher portion, their tem-
perature at that location also increased. As a result, the temperature of 
the furnace gradually increased. However, the overall furnace internal 
temperature decreased as the heat-absorbing coal gasification reactions 
(R4, R5) and solid-solid (R8) continued. This decrease in internal tem-
perature was attributed to other exothermic reactions, resulting in an 
insignificant decrease in temperature in the reactor. The temperature 
distribution was more uniform at 17–21 s, and the temperature change 
with time was insignificant because the reaction was close to its 
completion during this period. Before the material in the return circuit 
was replenished to the riser, the content of reactant particles in the riser 
was low, and the temperature became constant. This phenomenon 
indicated that the lignite CLG process with PG as OC proceeded rapidly 
under the mechanism of rapid fluidization. 
Fig. 10 shows the distribution of the gas temperature as a function of 
time. At 1–9 s, the gas temperature in the furnace was high, and the 
high-temperature region of the gas moved upward with time. During 
this time period, the temperature of the entire bed was roughly 
distributed in the range of 700–1600 K. This movement of the gas was 
attributed to the reaction of volatiles from lignite and oxygen in the OC 
at 1–9 s. The water vapor transition reaction (R2) also occurred at the 
same time, and these exothermic reactions increased the temperature of 
the region. As fluidized gas flowed in, the hot gas gradually blew to the 
top, and the high-temperature zone moved upward. At 11–15 s, the 
overall temperature in the furnace decreased. This decrease in the 
temperature was because the chemical reaction between coke and 
oxidized gas produced syngas, which is a heat-absorbing reaction, thus 
reducing the overall furnace temperature. At 17–21 s, a slight change in 
temperature was observed due to the delay in replenishing reactant 
lignite, indicating that the reaction in the riser was completed within 21 
s, and the whole process was fast, with a rapid rate of gas production. 
From Figs. 9 and 10, it can also be seen that the gas temperature was 
generally higher than the particle temperature until 7s. On the contrary 
after 9s, the particle temperature gradually exceeds the gas temperature.

3.2.2. Distribution of components in the riser 
Fig. 12 shows the spatial and temporal profiles of gas components in 
the riser. Generally, the reaction occurred faster, and the gaseous 
products were produced in large quantities in the middle and lower 
sections. Over time, the gas product was spread throughout the riser, 
and the collection zone gradually moved upward. CO was the major 
carbon conversion product due to the incomplete combustion of carbon 
[4]. The yield of CH4 was extremely low, consistent with our previous 
study [56]. During the first 5 s, CO was produced in large quantities and 
aggregated, and its overall volume distribution became progressively 
higher with time, whereas H2 gradually decreased in volume share after 
production. The occurrence of methane reforming reaction (R3), coal 
gasification reaction (R4), Boudouard reaction (R5), and solid-solid re-
action (R8) led to a large amount of CO generation. Even though H2 was 
generated through the simultaneous occurrence of the water vapor 
conversion reaction (R2), the methane reforming reaction (R3), and the 
coal gasification reaction (R4), it was at a disadvantage in the compe -
tition with R4, R5, and R8. Therefore, the volume share of H2 decreased 
relative to the CO production. In addition, in our previous reports [29], 
gas-solid reactions (R7) also played a role in this process, consuming H2. 
This process reinforced the idea that the contribution of CO to syngas 
yield was significant. Figs. 9 and 10 show that as the high-temperature 
region moves upward, CO gradually shifts upward in spatial distribu -
tion. This phenomenon was attributed to the upward movement of 
fluidizing wind and the favored movement of the heat-absorbing re-
actions (R3, R4, and R5) favored by the high temperature, increasing the 
concentration of CO close to the high-temperature region. Moreover, the 
high temperature contributed to the excitation of the active sites on the 
carbon surface [58,59], which also contributed to the solid-solid reac-
tion (R8). The result showed that even though the volume share of H2 in 
the whole riser gradually decreased, the region of H2 aggregation still 
shifted upward with the high-temperature region, mainly due to the 
contribution of reaction R3. This phenomenon was consistent with the 
study of Alam et al. [60]. They found that high temperatures indeed 
promote positive gas-solid reactions (R4 and R5). In contrast, the overall 
production of CO2 did not significantly change much within the first 5 s. The spatial distribution of CO and H2 shifted upward after 5 s, with a 
decrease in both the volume share. This phenomenon was because the 
gas generation caused a localized increase in pressure, and the gas was 
forced to flow upwards to the outlet. Meanwhile, the decrease in tem-
perature also influenced the generation of syngas [61]. In addition, 
elevated CO2 concentration depletes H radicals thereby decreasing H2 
concentration, as studied by Wijayanta et al. [62]. The CH4 yield was 
low throughout the process, which was also related to the presence of 
reaction R3 and the fact that the reaction was occurring efficiently at all 
times. This was similar to the results of a previous study by our group 
[56]. 
3.2.3. Heat transfer properties 
Because the CLG reaction mainly occurs in the riser, it is vital to 
comprehend the thermal properties of different bed heights in the riser, 
which can provide a theoretical basis for the optimal design of operating 
parameters and systems. The effect of particle size on the localized 
average HTC at axial heights in the riser is given in Fig. 13. As shown in 
Fig. 13(a), the HTC did not show clear monotonicity as the axis moved 
upward, but there was some volatility. The phenomenon was because 
the distribution of the bed in the riser was subjected to strong gas-solid 
mixing, and the inhomogeneity of the bed particle density led to drastic 
changes in the heat exchange. Generally, the HTC initially increased, 
reached a certain peak, and then declined. The peak generally occurred 
in the middle and upper regions. Under the influence of rapid fluidiza -
tion, most of the particles were rapidly transferred to the upper-middle 
region. The upper-middle region had a higher-density bed with a larger 
contact area for the particles, thus increasing HTC [63]. Several studies 
have shown that the heat transfer between the particles is mostly 
dominated by solid-solid conduction when the surface of solid particles 
is heated [64]. Therefore, the HTC is generally higher in the region with 
a higher solid volume fraction. Fewer unreacted particles in the bottom 
of the riser were observed when they were not circulated to the bottom. 
As a result, the contact area between the particles and the HTC became 
relatively small. In the upper area near the top, the material was also 
thinner, reducing the HTC. This typical particle flow pattern distribution 
has been similarly reported in the study of Zhu et al. [65]. This phe-
nomenon was because the material was mainly concentrated in the 
middle and upper part, and some particles in the area close to the top 
escaped from the riser as the fluidized air carried them away. 
Fig. 13(b) shows the influence of particle size on the average, min-
imum, and maximum values of HTC in the riser. The average value of 
HTC in the riser gradually increased as the particle size decreased from 
220 to 175 μm. The highest average HTC value was 180.6 W/(m2⋅K), 
indicating better particle mixing and bed mobility properties due to 
smaller particle size, which enhanced the heat transfer process. The 
finding was consistent with that of several researchers [42,63,66]. 
However, the average value of HTC slightly decreased as the particle size

4.Conclusion 
Three-dimensional numerical simulations of particle hydrodynamics 
in CFB and the CLG process of lignite were conducted using the Eulerian- 
Eulerian method. The established model was validated using relevant 
experimental data. Based on the simulation results, the following main 
conclusions were drawn: 
(1)The CFD simulation effectively captured the axial and radial ve-
locities of the particles. More radial flow occurred in the lower 
region of the riser and inside the cyclone, reflecting the violent 
particle collision in the riser and the excellent gas-solid separa -
tion in the cyclone. This process favored the gas-solid and solid- 
solid reactions. Particle concentration initially decreased and 
then increased in both riser and downcomer in a stably operating 
CFB.  
(2) The CLG reaction of lignite was complicated and rapid. The 
temperature inside the riser rapidly rose before 7 s, resulting in an 
uneven temperature distribution. The temperature inside the 
furnace decreased at 7–15 s. The temperature distribution in the 
furnace was not uniform until after 17 s. The exothermic reaction 
dominated the furnace within 7 s, while the heat-absorbing re-
action was dominant between 7 and 15 s. The reaction in the riser 
was almost complete at approximately 21 s before there was no 
material recharge. The main areas of CO and H2 production 
moved to the high-temperature zone. CO was the main contrib -
utor to syngas. The yield of CH4 production was low throughout 
the whole process. For better temperature distribution, 150 μm or 
175 μm should be selected.  
(3) Smaller particle sizes improved particle mixing and bed mobility 
properties, thus enhancing the heat transfer process. However, 
the dense bed with extremely small particle size affected the gas- 
solid mobility and suppressed the HTC values. The maximum 
average value of HTC (180.6 W/(m2⋅K)) was achieved at a par-
ticle size of 175 μm. The peak of HTC in the furnace occurred in 
the middle and upper regions, which was 387.7 W/(m2⋅K).
